The Tick Argument

Can a count of computation be money? Nine pre-registered experiments on a ternary machine, the constraint they end at, and the one way around it.

The claim, as posed

One tick is one unit of compute. Price a program by counting its ticks, and you have a currency whose unit means the same thing on every machine that ever runs it.

The appeal is real and it is easy to underrate. Every cost system in production prices work extrinsically — proof-of-work prices energy burnt, gas prices instructions executed — so the cost is not recoverable from the artifact and has to be attested by someone you trust. A tick is recoverable. Anyone holding the object recomputes the price by hand. There is nothing to attest and no meter to corrupt.

The second claim was stronger: that a tick count could serve as an implied receipt. If the only way to learn the number is to pay for it, then holding the number is the proof of payment, and physics does the enforcing — nobody can perform unbounded steps per second.

The first claim survived. The second was killed three separate times before it was rebuilt into something narrower and true — and then a reframing showed that the wall it ends at is a wall around receipts, not around minting.

What died, and how

A cost anyone can compute without doing the work cannot prove the work was done. That is nearly a tautology — and it took three measurements to discover that every version of the tick we had fell to it.

AB4 failed erasure is in the text

Predicted the thermodynamic cost of a program would be unrecoverable from its source. Six of eight programs charge exactly their text bound. The largest gap is 1.21×. The operand-dependent instructions simply don't appear in them, so the worst case is the actual case.

AC2 held — and that was worse two runs price two hundred

countdown's step count is exactly 5N + 3. Fit that from two executions and it predicts 200 of 200 inputs with zero error. Two runs price two hundred, and the ratio only improves with the sweep. Data-dependence alone establishes nothing.

AD2 held the patient predictor

Even a loop bound the program has to discover isn't enough. A memoised traversal resolves every one of 729 accumulator states in 728 evaluations, against 3,777 to answer the same questions by re-running. It breaks even at 70 queries and every answer after that is free.

A predictor is not obliged to be clever. It is allowed to be patient.

A price computed from a program's text cannot track what running it costs. Across that pair the two quantities are not even correlated.

So the cost is not in the shape, and is still cheap to obtain. You have to look at the operand values — and once you're evaluating operands, you have begun executing.

The four factors

Rebuilding the receipt took four attempts, and each one found a factor the last had missed. Only the first is about the size of the state space. The other three are about the shape of the map.

1

Reachable state count

The tabulator must index every state. Each live word of state multiplies its bill by 729 — 531,441 → 387 million → 282 billion. Three words puts it out of reach. Cheap to buy: 28 instructions.

2

Cost per evaluation overstated

A device-level solve costs 5,400 node-updates against 1 for an instruction. But it charges both sides — so the honest/attacker ratio gains 729× from state and exactly 1× from the work factor. It buys absolute infeasibility, not asymmetry.

3

Variance of the cost

A wide state whose cost never moves is security theatre. threeway has five live words and a spread of 1.02×; a tabulator would guess 55 and be right. countdown has one word and 25.38×. They are independent.

4

Trajectory convergence

The binding constraint. A control space 500,000× larger than before still handed the tabulator 3.20× reuse, because a subtractive loop merges onto shared suffixes. A tabulator eats convergence, not smallness.

A river system merges — many streams into one trunk, so a mapmaker surveys the trunk once and gets every tributary free. That is what a tabulator does, and factor four is what stops it.

The fourth factor, solved

And the answer had been sitting in the corpus for four sets, measured before anyone knew what it was for.

Take an n-input function as shift-register feedback: state (x₁…xₙ), next (x₂…xₙ, f(x₁…xₙ)). Over all 531,441 states of the two-input register:

Feedback functionMax in-degreeUnreachable
Reversible — one of the 216 1 0
MIN — collapsing 729 484,785  91.22%

A reversible feedback makes the state map a bijection — separate circular tracks, nothing ever joining anything. The tabulator's reuse is exactly 1.00. There is nothing to amortise.

A collapsing feedback is worse than a weak receipt. It leaves 91.22% of the state space with no predecessor at all — a space that mostly does not exist.

AI3 one rung of a closed form

An earlier set had swept all 19,683 two-input ternary tables one at a time and found 216 that preserve information. That turns out to be 6³, one rung of (2/9)3ⁿ⁻¹ — 22.22%, then 1.097%, then 0.000132%. The formula reproduces the sweep without sweeping.

Those 216 tables are exactly the feedback functions that make the fourth factor come out right — measured four sets before anyone knew what they were for.

And it is cheaper than that

The last set asked whether self-reference is what buys layer-one security. It isn't — and the property that does is free.

The strong version — a system closing over its own semantics — is provably unavailable, and the Ladder's own Coda III establishes it three separate ways: two of the three verdicts a normal-form function returns are not in the term. A dictionary can define every word using only words it defines. What it cannot do is tell you whether a definition bottoms out.

But carry the history operator in two words and the step is (sum, lag) → (sum + lag, sum + 1). Over all 531,441 states: maximum in-degree 1, zero unreachable. The inverse is explicit — sum = second − 1, lag = first − sum.

Every component is ADD, which erases zero bits. A map built of reversible parts is reversible. Not self-reference. Not even memory — a forgetful two-word carry has the property just as fully.

One set had to search 19,683 tables to find 216 that work. This needs nothing but addition.

Two catches, both measured

The reversibility is in the state, not the output. The operator collapses 81 histories onto 9 numbers, erasing 3.59 bits. The state map is a bijection and the emitted value is a nine-to-one channel simultaneously — different maps. Anyone reading only the number is reading an erasing channel.

And self-hosting does not inherit reversibility. The operator now runs inside the machine, in 152 instructions — and erases 6.26 bits per instruction, because it calls STORE every turn and STORE destroys 9.51 bits. A reversible mathematics on an erasing machine still erases.

The price of a trajectory graph that cannot converge is a state that cannot be bounded. Every real machine erases for exactly that reason.

The language it wants, in three forms

Make every primitive reversible and factor four stops being something you engineer per program. It becomes a property of the alphabet.

FormDoesNote

Fifteen instances on a three-trit register. Every one is a permutation and every one has order exactly 3 — so a gate's inverse is that gate applied twice, and the language needs no inverse instruction set at all. Together they generate A₂₇ = 27!/2, matching a figure recorded earlier from a separately written implementation.

bench 06

Write a program in it. Try to break the guarantee.

Fifteen gate instances on a three-trit register.

Two things are worth trying to break. The inverse is derived, not written: every gate has order three, so undoing a program is the same gates in reverse, each done twice — the language has no inverse instruction and does not need one. And in-degree stays 1 whatever you write, because a composition of bijections is a bijection. There is no program that breaks it, which is exactly why the guarantee belongs to the language rather than to a program.

AK2 factor four, free for every program

Four hundred random programs of random length. All 400 have in-degree exactly 1 at every state. A composition of bijections is a bijection — there is nothing a programmer can write that breaks it.

That matters because of where the guarantee lives. Earlier it was bought by searching 19,683 tables for the 216 that work — a property of a chosen table. Then by building a map deliberately out of ADD — a property of a particular program. Choosing a reversible alphabet makes it a property of the language, and only that version survives a programmer.

Which lands on a trilemma

Six constructions across this corpus, none with all three. Every pair is demonstrated; no triple is. That last row is Bennett's construction — a reversible machine can branch on data, but only by keeping the record of which way it went. The history then grows at least one trit per branch: a thousand data-dependent steps costs 167 trytes that can never be discarded.

In-degree one, a cost that varies, bounded state. Pick two.

This is a constraint, not a recipe — and that is the useful shape. A currency whose unit is compute can have a tabulator-proof trajectory graph, or a cost worth charging for, or a machine that fits in memory. Choosing which two is the design decision, and it is now a measured one rather than a matter of taste.

The question that isn't the same question

Everything above asks whether the cost of running something can be a receipt. A miner is not paid for running a program. It is paid for finding a rare ticket — and checking a ticket takes one glance.

The asymmetry is search against check, not run against run. A lottery ticket never claims a step count, so the trilemma does not bind it.

That is a genuine way past the wall rather than a way over it. In-degree, cost variance and bounded state are the constraints on a receipt. A search puzzle has different ones — and they run the other way.

So a candidate was built and measured exhaustively — all 531,441 message-and-nonce pairs at six trits, against a finite-population null model registered in advance, because over a bounded nonce space the naive 3ᵏ is simply wrong.

It broke three ways

AN2 held the tabulator, total this time

Against a program, the memoising predictor broke even at 70 queries. Against a puzzle it is not an approximation — a 729-entry inverse table solves 729 of 729 messages at one lookup each, at every difficulty. A 205× speedup.

And the affine candidate needs no table at all: the nonce is a rotation of a difference, so it is profitable on query one. Being affine is strictly worse than being merely reversible, and the gap between them is exactly the 729-entry table.

AN3 held irreversibility strands blocks

The puzzle needs compression, or the table above wins. But compression means some messages have no valid nonce anywhere — the target set sits outside the function's image and a miner holding one grinds forever.

At maximum difficulty 264 of 729 messages, 36.2%, cannot be mined at all. Both reversible controls strand exactly zero — so the property that guarantees every block is mineable is the same property that lets an attacker skip the mining.

AN1 failed and the failure caught the break

The registered test was that difficulty should vary across messages the way a lottery does. It came back too uniform — 0.729× and 0.674× where chance says 1.0×. A lottery fairer than chance is not a lottery; it is a machine with a lever.

The lever: the message entered through the same addition as the nonce, so it cancelled out. Every message was one puzzle, shifted. One 729-entry index then solved every mineable message in 1.54 lookups against a brute force of up to 305.

bench 04

The column that has no business being constant.

Six different blocks, six different nonces, chosen only so that message ⊕ nonce comes to the same value. Subtract the message back off each digest and read the last column down.

Three by hand, then the whole space at once.

When the message is added to the input, it cancels: the digest is a function of message ⊕ nonce alone, plus a shift. Every block is the same puzzle wearing a different hat, and an index of 729 entries mines all of them. Switch to keying the mixer and the column breaks up — which is the fix, and the reason to believe the diagnosis.

Then the confirmation, which is the part that makes it a finding rather than a guess. Rebuild it so the message steers the mixer instead of being added to its input, and the deviation vanishes — within 3% of chance at every difficulty, and the cancellation is gone.

A fix that removes the symptom is what confirms the cause. The prediction that failed is the one that detected the break.

Three design rules, measured rather than borrowed

RuleWhat was measured
The message must steer the machinery, not be added to the input Otherwise it commutes out and every block is one puzzle. Verified on all 531,441 pairs.
Irreversibility is paid for in stranded blocks 36.2% unmineable at maximum difficulty — and the number survives a full rebuild, so it is about compression, not this construction.
The cure is more nonce room, not a better function A wider nonce drove stranding to 0 and corrected the search cost from 311.2 to 735.0 — within 0.8% of the 729 a proper lottery owes.

That last rule is what Bitcoin's extraNonce field is for. Here it arrives as a measurement rather than a citation — and the stranding and the shortcut turned out to be one object: the mixer's image is 465 of 729 digests, and 729 − 465 = 264 is exactly the stranded count. Nothing predicted the two numbers would be the same number.

Mint-by-search is live where mint-by-receipt is dead. What is still missing is a way to certify a mixer rather than merely fail to break it.

The usable candidate set was already counted, from the other direction, before anyone asked: 19,458 of 19,683 two-input ternary operations — every one that is neither affine nor reversible. One refutation test now exists and it works. It is one test at 729 states, and a toy is not evidence against an adversary.

Where it actually stands

Four primitives make a layer one. One is nearly built, one is genuinely novel, and two are not close.

🗺️ Distributed ledger Closest. Content-addressed, append-only, tamper-evident — and a live reorg was handled correctly mid-session. But the machine has no clock: every state the solver returns is a fixed point, so ordering must be imported from outside.
🤝 Consensus The novel part. Eleven opcodes erase nothing and belong to a cache anyone can recompute; nine destroy something and need agreement. The consensus surface shrinks to the erasures, measured per instruction. Sybil resistance is still absent, and no cost function provides it.
⛓️ Hashing Raw material yes — ternary hands you nonlinearity for free where binary has none. Collision resistance no. The obvious test was disqualified: truncating any permutation is uniform, so a map proven to repeat every six calls scored 0.96× ideal. A refutation test now exists — hit-count variance, which reads exactly zero for a relabelling and caught a real break — but it refutes rather than certifies, and that gap is still open.
🗝️ Keys Nothing — and the tempting construction is ruled out by our own tooling. Group membership is solved efficiently by the same stabiliser chain that made these results measurable. We built the attacker before the puzzle.

The spec, corrected

In priority order, with two items demoted from where they started.

#Do thisBecause
1 Make the cost vary Non-negotiable and first. Everything else is worthless without it — 513.8× achieved, against 1.02× for a five-word program.
2 Reversible feedback In-degree 1 everywhere, so nothing merges. Free — it only has to be built out of addition.
3 Widen the live state ×729 per word, and it costs the honest party nothing. Three words in 28 instructions.
4 Device-level tick Demoted. Buys absolute cost, not asymmetry — it charges both sides equally and cancels from the ratio.

And the honest framing of what a tick is: a very good unit of account and a very good gas metric. Deterministic, recomputable, identical on every machine. It is not, and cannot be made into, a proof that anyone paid — unless the four factors above are all satisfied at once, which is a thing you engineer rather than a thing you hope for.

That is the spec for pricing. Issuance is a separate problem with a separate answer, and the four factors are not its constraints: a puzzle is scored on whether a shortcut exists, not on whether a step count is recoverable. Which is why the reframing above is not a refinement of this list. It replaces it.

Two more benches, from the last sitting

The ledger question and the energy floor both arrived after this page was first written, and both are the kind that read better turned than described.

bench 08

Forge the ledger. Two edits that cancel.

Six entries, mints and burns. Add δ to one and subtract it from another — the kind of edit that mints to yourself and burns from someone else.

The summed head is the history operator. The hashed head is the mixer.

A ledger’s job is not to be reversible — it is to make tampering detectable. Both heads carry the same 9.51 bits about the whole ledger and have the same number of collisions. The difference is that the sum hands you one by solving a linear equation.

bench 09

The floor under the price of a hash.

SHA-256 takes 768 bits and emits 256, so it must destroy 512.

A hash must erase, or `AN2` inverts it with a 729-entry table. Erasure costs kT ln 2 a bit. So the energy floor and the security are one property — you cannot make a hash cheaper than its erasure without making it invertible. Note the distance, though: real hardware sits about 6.7 orders of magnitude above this. Physics is real and is nowhere near binding.

What the failures did

Thirty-three of 185 predictions failed, and they did most of the work. The conclusions that matter are, without exception, the ones that arrived by a prediction being wrong.

Twenty-one instrument failures were caught along the way, and one transcription slip landed inside a registration where it could not be corrected — a 28-digit number typed with three zeros missing. It cost nothing only because the tool computes the factorial rather than trusting the digits beside it. The most instructive pointed toward the hypothesis: a tabulator reported as 300× worse than brute force, which would have made the receipt look sound. It was a guard counted as work — the same mechanism that had already bitten once, in the same thread, after being written down.

An instrument erring in the direction you are hoping for will never be caught by finding the answer surprising, because it is not surprising.

The seventeenth is the mirror image, and it says what the remedy has to be. An attack tool shifted every nonce it produced by a constant and reported solving 0 of 729 — which reads perfectly well on its own, since a structured puzzle resisting a structural attack is a sensible-sounding result. Nothing about it looked wrong. What caught it was that a different measurement had already counted 465 of those same messages as solvable.

Neither a plausible number nor a careful reading would have found it. Two measurements of one quantity, forced to agree, did.
